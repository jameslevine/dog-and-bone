When parents hand their child a smartphone, they rarely think of it as handing them a device that has been engineered by teams of psychologists to be as difficult to put down as possible. But that is precisely what it is.

The science on children and screen time has been building for a decade, and the picture it paints is concerning. **Children who spend more time on smartphones — particularly on social media — show higher rates of anxiety, depression, sleep disruption, and attention difficulties.** The youngest users are the most vulnerable, and the effects appear to be cumulative.

Understanding why requires understanding a little about how these platforms work, and how young brains respond to them.

## What the AAP Actually Says

The American Academy of Pediatrics has issued screen time guidelines that have become increasingly specific as the research has matured:

- **Under 18 months:** No screen time except video calls with family
- **18–24 months:** Limited to high-quality programming, watched with a parent
- **2–5 years:** Maximum one hour per day of high-quality content
- **6 and older:** Consistent limits on time and type of media; screens should not displace sleep, physical activity, or face-to-face interaction

What the AAP is most concerned about is not screens per se — it is **displacement.** Every hour a child spends on a device is an hour not spent on the activities that build healthy brains: unstructured play, face-to-face socialising, reading, physical movement, and sleep.

A smartphone handed to a ten-year-old is not a neutral object. It competes directly with all of these things, and — because it has been engineered to be compelling — it usually wins.

## Dopamine and the Developing Brain

The brain's reward system is centred on dopamine, a neurotransmitter associated with motivation and pleasure. When something good happens — we eat something tasty, we receive a compliment, we win a game — dopamine is released, and we feel good. The brain remembers what triggered the release, and we seek out that trigger again.

Social media platforms have been deliberately designed to trigger dopamine release as frequently and unpredictably as possible. Likes, comments, notifications, and new content all deliver small dopamine hits. **The unpredictability is key** — slot machines release dopamine more effectively than predictable rewards because the brain is wired to pay extra attention to things it can't predict.

The problem for children is that their brains are still developing the prefrontal cortex — the part responsible for impulse control, long-term thinking, and the ability to resist temptation. This development isn't complete until the mid-twenties. Children and teenagers are therefore uniquely ill-equipped to resist the platforms that have been designed to defeat adult willpower.

Repeated heavy stimulation of the dopamine system during childhood may also raise the baseline level of stimulation required to feel interested or engaged. Activities that don't deliver rapid, unpredictable rewards — reading a book, sitting with a problem, having a conversation — can start to feel unbearably boring in comparison.

## Sleep: The Hidden Casualty

One of the clearest effects of smartphone use in children is disrupted sleep. The mechanisms are multiple:

**Blue light exposure** from screens suppresses melatonin production, delaying the onset of sleep even when the phone has been put away.

**Cognitive and emotional activation** from social media content keeps the brain in an alert, engaged state that is not conducive to sleep.

**Notifications** during the night cause fragmented sleep even in children who have fallen asleep.

And critically, **the fear of missing out** keeps many children lying awake checking their phone rather than sleeping.

Children who are chronically sleep-deprived show impaired attention, memory, learning, and emotional regulation — the exact same symptoms that are independently attributed to heavy social media use. Some researchers believe that the majority of the academic and behavioural impact attributed to screens actually comes through the sleep disruption pathway.

## The "But All Their Friends Have It" Problem

The most common objection parents raise is the social argument: if all of their friends are on Instagram and your child isn't, won't they be left out?

This is a real concern and deserves a real answer.

The first point is that the social infrastructure of teenage life has, to a concerning degree, moved onto commercial platforms designed to monetise it. This is something that has happened to us collectively, not something any individual family chose. The solution is not to pretend it hasn't happened.

But the second point is equally important: **belonging to a social group does not require being in the same attention-harvesting environment as that group.** Your child can still have a phone, still message their friends on WhatsApp, still be reachable. What they won't have is the endless content feed, the public performance of identity, and the comparison anxiety that social platforms generate.

Many families who have made this switch report that their children initially protest and then — within weeks — are genuinely relieved. The anxiety associated with maintaining a social media presence, keeping up with content, managing how you're perceived online: most children don't enjoy it. They do it because everyone else does.

**Being the family that opts out can, over time, become a source of identity rather than a source of shame.**
