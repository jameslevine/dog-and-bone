The call comes on a Sunday afternoon. Your mum has accidentally activated voice assistant again and can't figure out how to turn it off. Or your dad's phone has updated overnight and all his icons have moved and now he can't find his contacts. Or your elderly aunt has somehow ended up with six apps open and the battery is flat by 10am.

Sound familiar? You're not alone.

Millions of adult children find themselves acting as unpaid tech support for elderly parents who have been given smartphones designed for twenty-five-year-olds with fast reflexes and a decade of touchscreen experience. The parents feel anxious and incompetent. The children feel guilty about not being more helpful. And the phone — the smartphone that was supposed to connect everyone — becomes a source of stress rather than comfort.

The problem is not the person. The problem is the phone.

## Why Smartphones Are Genuinely Difficult for Seniors

Modern smartphones are not intuitive. They feel intuitive to people who grew up with them, but that is because those people have absorbed years of implicit knowledge about how they work. The assumption that everything is a tap, the knowledge that you can swipe to go back, the understanding of what the little icons mean, the familiarity with the concept of an app store — none of this is obvious. All of it is learned.

For elderly users who came to smartphones later in life, these conventions can be genuinely baffling. But beyond the learning curve, there are specific design choices that create unnecessary difficulty:

**Small touch targets.** Many smartphones have icons, buttons, and text that are too small for people with reduced fine motor control or impaired vision.

**Complex menus.** The settings menu on a modern smartphone is a labyrinth. Finding something as simple as making the text bigger requires navigating through multiple levels of menus with confusing labels.

**Accidental triggers.** Features like face ID, fingerprint sensors, swipe gestures, and voice assistants frequently trigger accidentally, confusing users and changing settings they didn't mean to change.

**Updates.** Operating system updates change the interface. Apps update and their layouts shift. Something that worked last week doesn't work the same way this week. For someone who has finally learned a routine, this is profoundly disorienting.

**Too many choices.** A modern smartphone comes with dozens of pre-installed apps, settings menus with hundreds of options, and an app store with millions of downloads. Choice paralysis is a real cognitive phenomenon, and it affects everyone — but seniors are particularly susceptible when combined with anxiety about "getting it wrong."

## What Elderly Users Actually Need

Strip away the marketing and the features, and what an elderly person actually needs from a phone is quite specific:

- **To make and receive calls** with the people they love
- **To send and receive messages** (SMS and WhatsApp)
- **To video call** grandchildren and family members who live far away
- **To access maps** when they're out and about
- **To take photographs** of their garden, grandchildren, and holidays
- **Emergency features** — a way to call for help if something goes wrong
- **Confidence** that they won't accidentally break something

That last point is perhaps the most underappreciated. **Anxiety about doing something wrong is one of the biggest barriers to technology adoption for older adults.** A phone that has fewer ways to go wrong is a phone that feels safer to use.

## Why Giving Them a Flagship iPhone Doesn't Work

There's an instinct among caring adult children to give their parents the very best phone available. An iPhone 16 or a Samsung Galaxy S25 — something top of the range that will last a long time and has all the features they could ever want.

The problem is that these devices come loaded with complexity that actively works against elderly users. The very features that make them flagship devices — advanced gesture controls, edge-to-edge screens with accidental touch issues, complex camera systems with multiple modes, always-on AI assistants — are sources of confusion and frustration rather than delight.

**More features does not mean a better phone for an elderly user. It means more things to go wrong.**

## The Dignity Argument

There's a conversation worth having about the way we talk about elderly people and technology. Too often, the narrative is one of deficit — "they just can't figure it out," "they're not tech people," "it's too complicated for them."

This framing places the problem with the person rather than with the technology. **A phone that is genuinely difficult to use is a badly designed phone.** The failure is with the design, not the user.

Dog and Bone's Senior profile is not a "dumbed down" phone. It is a thoughtfully designed phone that removes unnecessary complexity so that the essential functions work beautifully. That is good design, full stop.

They deserve a phone that makes them feel capable, not anxious.
