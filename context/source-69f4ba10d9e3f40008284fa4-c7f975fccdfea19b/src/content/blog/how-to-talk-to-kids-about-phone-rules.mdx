The conversation about phone rules is one that most parents dread. It often happens reactively — after you've noticed your twelve-year-old is on their phone until 1am, or after a teacher has called about phone use in class, or after a tearful incident involving something on Instagram that you're only getting part of the story about.

Reactive conversations are hard because they happen when emotions are already high and trust may already be strained. The best conversations about phone rules are **proactive, thoughtful, and ideally involve the child in creating the rules rather than having them imposed.**

This is a guide to having those conversations well, at different ages, and to the Dog and Bone approach that removes the need for many of the ongoing arguments that phone rules typically generate.

## Understanding What Your Child Actually Uses Their Phone For

Before setting any rules, it's worth genuinely understanding your child's relationship with their phone — not just the surface behaviour, but what function it serves.

For most children, the phone is doing several things simultaneously:

- **Social maintenance:** Keeping up with friends, group chats, the social life that now partly happens online
- **Stimulation and entertainment:** Videos, games, content to fill idle time
- **Identity exploration:** Social media as a space to present yourself and receive feedback
- **Escape:** A retreat from family life, from boredom, from anxiety
- **Genuine information:** Homework help, music, things they're actually interested in

Some of these functions are legitimate and worth supporting. Some are problematic. A good phone conversation starts by **taking seriously the legitimate functions** rather than treating all phone use as the enemy.

Ask your child: what would you miss if you didn't have your phone? What are the things you actually value? This question, asked genuinely and listened to seriously, often yields a surprisingly honest answer. Most children know that the hours of mindless scrolling aren't serving them well — they've just never been asked to say so out loud.

## Age-Appropriate Guidelines

### Under 10: Not Yet

Children under ten don't need smartphones. A basic handset that can make calls to parents and a short list of trusted contacts is more than sufficient for the safety and communication needs of a child in this age group.

If your child under ten has a smartphone, the most valuable thing you can do is replace it with something simpler. This is much easier than it sounds, because children under ten are less socialised into the expectation that they should have a smartphone than older children are.

### Age 10–12: Basic Communication

Children in this age group may benefit from a phone for genuine communication and safety reasons, particularly as they begin to travel to school independently or have more independent social lives.

The key principle for this age group: **the phone is a communication tool, not an entertainment device.**

This means:

- Calls and SMS only, or calls/SMS plus WhatsApp for family group chats
- No social media
- No YouTube or video platforms
- No gaming apps
- No app store access
- Phone used in common areas of the house, not in bedrooms

A Dog and Bone Family profile is ideal for this age group. The configuration enforces these limits at the hardware level, so the conversation is about why the phone is set up this way rather than about enforcement.

### Age 13–15: Communication Plus Expansion

Early teenagers have legitimate needs for more phone functionality than younger children. They may need maps, they may have more independent social lives, they may be using music and podcasts for studying.

The research on social media and mental health is most relevant to this age group — the 11–14 window is when girls are most vulnerable to the effects of Instagram and similar platforms.

**Dog and Bone's recommendation for this age group:** a Family profile that includes communication, maps, camera, and music, but still excludes social media and video platforms.

This will likely generate protest. The script for that conversation is: "We've read the research about what Instagram and TikTok do to teenage mental health, particularly for girls your age. These platforms are designed by very clever people to make you feel bad about yourself while keeping you looking at them. We love you too much to give you access to them yet."

Age-appropriate phone freedoms to consider introducing at 13–15:

- A safe, filtered browser with expanding permissions over time
- YouTube access with usage time limits (on a shared device, not the phone)
- Social media awareness: discuss how these platforms work, the business model, the psychology

### Age 16–18: Graduated Trust

At 16, many parents feel comfortable beginning to restore access to social media and other platforms. If you've had the conversation about how these platforms work, your teenager is better equipped to use them critically.

The goal by this age is not restriction but **critical awareness.** Your teenager should understand:

- That engagement metrics (likes, views, followers) are business metrics, not measures of their worth
- That the algorithm is not their friend — it shows them content designed to provoke emotional reactions
- That comparison on social media is comparison with a curated fiction
- That their screen time data is information worth paying attention to

Consider transitioning to a phone with some social media access at 16, while maintaining open conversations about usage. Many families find that children who have had a Dog and Bone phone for several years are genuinely less susceptible to the addictive patterns of social media because they've spent their formative years without it.

## Practical Rules Worth Having

Beyond the phone configuration, some household agreements are valuable regardless of what device your child has:

**Phones off at mealtimes.** This applies to everyone at the table, including parents. If you're on your phone at dinner, you've already lost the argument.

**Phones charged outside the bedroom.** The evidence for sleep disruption from phones in bedrooms is overwhelming. A family charging station in a common area, established as the norm before children have smartphones, is far easier to maintain than trying to introduce it after.

**Phone-free time zones.** Homework time, family time, the hour before bed. These don't need to be policed — they need to be modelled. Children who see their parents with boundaries around phone use are more willing to have their own.

**Weekly check-ins.** Not monitoring, just conversation. "How's your phone feeling this week? Anything you want to talk about?" These regular low-stakes conversations create the environment where your child will come to you when something goes wrong online.

## The Dog and Bone Approach to Enforcement

One of the reasons parents find phone rules so exhausting is that they require constant enforcement. Rules are set, rules are broken, conversations happen, rules are reset, rules are broken again. The cycle is demoralising for everyone.

Dog and Bone's approach removes a large portion of this cycle by making the rules structural rather than reliant on willpower or trust. **The Family profile is not a list of rules to follow — it is a phone that simply doesn't have the apps that cause the problems.**

This shifts the conversation. Instead of "did you go on Instagram last night?" the question is "how is your phone working for you?" It moves from enforcement to relationship.

Children who understand why their phone is configured the way it is — who have been given the research and treated with the respect of an honest explanation — are almost universally more accepting of the configuration than children who feel it has been imposed arbitrarily.

**You're not restricting their phone. You're giving them a phone that respects their brain.**
