When we told people we were going to call our minimalist phone company "Dog and Bone," the responses fell into two distinct categories. British people laughed and immediately understood. Everyone else looked confused.

That reaction, we decided, was exactly right for what we were building.

## The Origins of Cockney Rhyming Slang

Cockney rhyming slang emerged in the East End of London in the mid-nineteenth century, somewhere around the 1840s. Theories about its precise origin vary — some historians believe it developed as a way for market traders and street criminals to communicate without being understood by the police; others think it was simply a creative linguistic game that spread virally through the dense, interconnected communities of working-class Victorian London.

The mechanism is elegant in its silliness: find a phrase that rhymes with the word you want to say, then — crucially — drop the part that actually rhymes, leaving only the first word. So "stairs" becomes "apples and pears" becomes "apples." Your feet are your "plates" (plates of meat). Your eyes are your "mincers" (mince pies). Your head is your "loaf" (loaf of bread). Your wife is your "trouble" (trouble and strife).

**And your phone — your telephone — is your "dog and bone."**

Except by the time the telephone existed, Cockney rhyming slang had already been evolving for decades. The first telephones arrived in British homes in the 1870s and 1880s, and somewhere in the East End, someone decided that the ringing, barking device demanded a rhyming name. Dog and bone. Perfect.

## Why It Felt Right

When we were naming this company, we went through the usual process of brand strategy consultancy-speak: brand pillars, positioning statements, competitive differentiation. We sat in rooms and wrote things on whiteboards.

And then someone said, "what if we just called it what it is?" — and someone else said, "it's a dog and bone" — and the room went quiet in the way rooms do when something is obviously right.

The name Dog and Bone works on several levels that we think are genuinely meaningful.

**It's human.** It comes from the street, from the vernacular, from people talking to each other in markets and pubs. It has none of the synthetic Silicon Valley naming energy of "ConnectX" or "PhonePlus" or whatever seven-letter made-up word with a missing vowel is currently popular in tech branding. It sounds like something a person would say.

**It's British.** We're a British company. We believe in the British tradition of making things that are well-made and unpretentious, of valuing substance over spectacle, of having a bit of a laugh. British culture has a healthy scepticism of things that take themselves too seriously — which is useful when you're building something that is, at its core, a poke in the eye to the most powerful technology companies in the world.

**It reminds you what a phone actually is.** A dog and bone is just a phone. Not a portal to infinite distraction. Not a social performance platform. Not a dopamine delivery mechanism. A phone. A thing you use to talk to people. The name is a gentle reminder every time you pick it up.

**It's got a bit of joy in it.** The minimalist phone space, like the digital wellness space generally, can be very earnest and serious. People write long, anxious articles about screen time and mental health (we've written some of them). The technology critique genre can feel like reading a particularly thorough list of reasons to feel bad. We wanted our name to be something that makes people smile. Dog and bone. It's a bit daft, in the best possible way.

## The British Identity of the Brand

Our Britishness is not an affectation. It shapes how we think about the problem we're solving.

**British culture values understatement.** We find the superlatives of American consumer marketing ("Revolutionary." "Iconic." "Change everything.") slightly embarrassing. A phone that does less by doing more does not need to tell you it's changing your life — it just gets on with doing it.

**British culture has a specific relationship with technology.** We were not the country that invented the smartphone or the social media platform. We are often the country that looks at the American invention and asks, after a decent interval: "was that actually a good idea?" There is a British tradition of the critical outsider that we find comfortable to occupy.

**British culture makes room for humour about serious things.** The UK has some of the highest rates of problematic social media use in the world. We are talking about a genuine public health issue. And yet we named our company after Victorian street slang for a phone. Because sometimes the best way to communicate the absurdity of a situation is with a light touch.

## "Simple Phone. Happy Life."

Our tagline was arrived at by the same process as the name: not through brand strategy, but through honesty.

We kept asking ourselves: what do people actually want? Not the things they say in focus groups or in answer to survey questions, but the thing underneath those answers?

They want to feel better. They want to feel less anxious, less distracted, less like they're wasting their life. They want to be present for the things that matter. They want a phone that is on their side.

**Simple phone. Happy life.** That's it. That's the whole thing.

The simplicity of the phone is the mechanism. The happiness is the outcome. The life is yours.

We're a funny little British company selling a phone that does less and calling it by the name of a 170-year-old piece of street slang. We think that's rather marvellous.
