I want to be honest with you about something. Before I switched to a Dog and Bone phone, I was spending, on average, five hours and seventeen minutes per day on my smartphone. I know this with unsettling precision because I checked my screen time report obsessively — which added a few more minutes to the total, presumably.

Of that time, roughly three hours was what I would loosely call "scrolling." Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), YouTube, Reddit, back to Instagram, a quick check of the news, back to YouTube. I was not seeking anything in particular. I was not entertained, exactly, or informed. I was just... in the current. Carried along.

I knew I had a problem. I'd known for years. I'd tried the screen time limits (I always dismissed them), the grayscale display (I turned it off after two days), the "phone-free bedroom" rule (broken every night within a week). I'd read the books. I'd listened to the podcasts about phone addiction, on my phone.

Nothing changed. Until the phone changed.

## Week One: The Withdrawal

I'm going to be honest: the first few days were uncomfortable.

The Dog and Bone Balance profile arrived on a Tuesday. I charged it overnight, slid my SIM in on Wednesday morning, and — after a brief moment of mourning — left my old phone in a drawer.

The new phone had everything I needed: WhatsApp, a calling app, the Google Maps, my banking app, the camera, a podcast app, a music streaming service. What it didn't have was Instagram, YouTube, Reddit, X, or the news apps. The app store was locked. There was nothing to install.

The first morning was strange. **I kept picking up the phone expecting to find something and finding nothing.** There was no feed to scroll. There were no notifications to dismiss. When I opened the phone, it sat there quietly, offering me exactly what I'd asked for and nothing more.

My brain was looking for its dopamine. It kept sending little signals: _check Instagram, check Twitter, something interesting is probably happening._ I would reach for my phone, feel the smooth surface, open it, and have absolutely nothing to do. It was, in the first few days, slightly maddening.

By Thursday of the first week, I noticed I was eating lunch without my phone. Not deliberately — I'd just started eating, and the phone wasn't there, and I hadn't reached for it because I knew there was nothing on it to reach for. I sat and ate my lunch and looked out the window and thought about things. It felt almost transgressive.

The anxiety was real, though. I can't pretend otherwise. A low-level hum of "what am I missing" that I had to consciously sit with rather than dispel by checking. This, I later read, is withdrawal from a genuine neurological habit loop. The brain doesn't distinguish between the dopamine hit from Instagram and the dopamine hit from heroin — both are signals that travel the same reward pathways. Withdrawing from either creates real discomfort.

## Week Two: The Adjustment

By the second week, something started to shift.

The anxiety about missing things diminished. Not because I stopped caring about the world, but because I realised — with increasing clarity — that the things I'd been "missing" when I was off my phone were almost entirely worthless. The hot takes on X. The memes on Reddit. The sponsored posts on Instagram. None of it had any value. I had been suffering anxiety about missing content that did not improve my life in any measurable way.

**This sounds obvious. It sounds like something you would know without having to experience it. But knowing it and feeling it are completely different things.** I had known it for years. I'd read the books. I hadn't really felt the pointlessness of it until the option to consume it was gone.

In week two, I started noticing what I was doing instead. I was reading. Not articles on my phone — actual books, which I'd been buying and failing to read for a year. I finished one in three days. I started another.

I was having longer conversations. Not because I was more interested in people, but because there was nothing competing for my attention. I'd sit across from someone at dinner and the phone wouldn't call to me, and I'd actually listen, and the conversation would go somewhere interesting.

I was sleeping better. Within ten days of getting the new phone, I was falling asleep roughly forty minutes earlier. I hadn't changed anything about my bedtime routine. I had simply stopped spending forty minutes scrolling in bed before switching the light off.

## Month One: The Realisation

A month in, I sat down to think about what had actually changed.

The big things were measurable. **I was reading approximately three books per month** where I had previously managed perhaps one every two months. I was sleeping an average of forty-five minutes longer per night. I had taken up running — not because a phone app told me to, but because I suddenly had the mental space to notice that I was sedentary and wanted not to be. I had started cooking properly at weekends instead of watching videos about cooking while eating something delivered.

The subtler changes were harder to measure but felt more significant.

I felt _less reactive._ The news cycle, social media outrage, the constant stream of things to be concerned or amused or annoyed about — I was no longer being marinated in it all day. My baseline emotional state calmed noticeably. I wasn't less informed about the world — I checked the news once a day on my laptop — but I was no longer in a state of constant low-level alarm.

I had opinions that were my own again. When you're fed a steady diet of other people's takes, your thoughts start to feel less like your thoughts and more like a remix of what you've consumed. Removing that feed gave back a certain quietness of mind that I hadn't realised I'd lost.

I was present. This is the word that comes up most in accounts of people reducing their phone use, and it sounds like a cliché, but it describes something real. I was **in the room.** When something was happening, I was watching it rather than thinking about documenting it, or half-watching it while reading something on my phone.

## What I Gained

I want to be specific about this, because "I found myself" is not useful information.

- Roughly **35 extra hours per month** of waking time that was previously spent scrolling
- **3 books read** in the first month (I hadn't finished a book in six months previously)
- **Better sleep** — falling asleep faster, waking less in the night
- **A running habit** — I completed a 5k by month two
- **Lower anxiety** — particularly the ambient "what am I missing" feeling that had become background noise
- **Richer conversations** — people noticed I was more present; a few commented on it directly

What I did not lose:

- Contact with everyone who matters to me (WhatsApp kept working fine)
- Navigation (Google Maps worked perfectly)
- Music (podcast and music apps were on the phone)
- The ability to take photos (the A12 camera is genuinely good)
- My job (which requires a computer, not a social media feed)

## The Honest Assessment

I'm not going to tell you it was seamless. There were moments of genuine FOMO, particularly around news events where everyone seemed to be discussing something I hadn't seen. There were a couple of social situations where an Instagram story was being discussed and I had nothing to contribute.

These moments were real. They were also, on reflection, minor — and they diminished over time as the people around me adjusted to the fact that I wasn't on these platforms, and as I cared less about contributing to conversations I hadn't been invited into.

**The discomfort of week one was worth the transformation of month three.** I say month three because that's how long it took to feel completely natural. By then, the old phone in the drawer had lost its appeal entirely.
