YouTube is different from other social media platforms. People know Instagram makes them feel bad — they use it anyway, but they know. YouTube is harder to criticise because so much of the content is genuinely educational and genuinely entertaining. You can absolutely defend your YouTube use: you watch documentaries, tutorials, comedy shows, music. This is real. YouTube contains valuable content.

The problem is not the content. The problem is the interface.

YouTube's recommendation algorithm is one of the most powerful and least discussed persuasion systems in human history. It has one job: to find the next video that keeps you watching. It has access to hundreds of signals — your watch history, what you pause and rewind, what thumbnails your eyes dwell on, how your engagement patterns change throughout the day — and it uses all of them to predict, with remarkable accuracy, what you will click on next.

The result is that watching one video on YouTube has a very low correlation with watching one video on YouTube. The algorithm is always there with the next thing. And the next thing is specifically chosen to be more compelling than what you just watched.

I spend, according to my screen time data, an average of one hour and forty-two minutes per day on YouTube. This is — I will be honest — more than I spend on any other activity except sleeping and working.

I decided to remove it for 30 days and keep detailed notes.

## Day One: The Immediate Reflex

I deleted the YouTube app at 8am on a Monday. By 9am, I had opened my phone three times with the intention of watching YouTube before remembering I'd deleted it.

This was instructive. I wasn't bored. I wasn't specifically seeking out something to watch. I was responding to a habit loop that had been reinforced thousands of times over years: phone in hand, open YouTube. The action was nearly autonomous.

The third time this happened, I sat with it for a moment instead of immediately doing something else. And I noticed something: **I didn't actually want to watch anything.** I didn't have a specific video in mind. I wasn't looking for information or entertainment with any particular need behind it. I was just reaching for a stimulus because my brain had learned that a stimulus was available.

## Week One: The Void and What It Reveals

The first week was characterised by a restless, slightly itchy feeling whenever I had unoccupied time. Waiting for a bus. Eating lunch alone. A gap between tasks. These had all been automatically filled with YouTube, and now they weren't.

In those gaps, two things happened.

First, I noticed how many of my "breaks" had actually been YouTube sessions. I thought I took brief five-minute breaks between tasks. My screen time data told a different story — those breaks were more often twenty or thirty minutes of YouTube. **I had genuinely not noticed the time passing.** The "just one more video" effect is so smooth, so well-engineered, that the experience of losing time to it is not like being in a trance. It just feels like time passing normally.

Second, I noticed what occupied the gaps when YouTube wasn't available. My mind wandered. I thought about things — not usefully or productively, just in the undirected way that minds do when they're not being directed. I'd forgotten what this felt like. It was uncomfortable for the first few days and then, gradually, pleasant.

By the end of week one, I was reading while eating lunch.

## Week Two: The Recalibration

In the second week, something interesting happened with my relationship to boredom.

I had assumed, going in, that the difficulty would be not having something to watch when I wanted to be entertained. But I found that my desire to be entertained was itself declining. The constant stimulation of the YouTube algorithm had, without my noticing, raised my baseline threshold for what counted as entertaining. I needed novelty, movement, escalation.

Without the algorithm feeding that appetite, **the appetite itself started to diminish.** I read three chapters of a book in a single afternoon and found it genuinely engaging — something I couldn't have done a week earlier because my attention had been calibrated for four-minute videos.

I also started to notice what I was actually interested in, as opposed to what the algorithm thought I was interested in. YouTube had been feeding me videos adjacent to my interests — close enough to feel relevant, optimised for engagement rather than value. Without it, I found myself going back to things I actually cared about: listening to music properly, reading about topics I genuinely wanted to understand, watching one specific documentary I'd been meaning to see for years, on my laptop, from beginning to end.

## Week Three: The Substitution Effect

The most surprising discovery of the month was how naturally the reclaimed time filled with other things.

I had expected to feel deprived. I didn't. Here is what I did instead, tracked over the two weeks following week one:

**Reading:** I read two and a half books in the month. In the prior month, with YouTube, I read none.

**Podcasts:** I started listening to long-form interviews and narrative podcasts during commutes and exercise, which I'd previously spent watching YouTube shorts. The quality of the information I was consuming increased dramatically.

**Actual rest:** I discovered I had been using YouTube as a substitute for rest — stimulation instead of restoration. Without it, I started taking genuine breaks: lying on the sofa and doing nothing, sitting in the garden, going for short walks without a destination.

**Cooking:** I had been watching cooking videos for years without, somehow, doing much actual cooking. Freed of the videos, I started cooking. The food I made was considerably less impressive than the food in the videos, which was fine.

## The Algorithm's Footprint

By week four, I was thinking about something that had been nagging at me throughout the experiment.

YouTube had been shaping my interests and opinions without my awareness. The algorithm doesn't just recommend what you've already shown interest in — it recommends **escalations** of what you've shown interest in, because escalation drives engagement. A mild interest in history might be nudged toward more dramatic history content, then toward more contrarian takes on history, then toward something genuinely ideological, all through a sequence of individually small steps that felt like your own curiosity.

I had spent years consuming content that felt like it reflected my interests and values. But how much of it had I actively sought out, and how much had been placed in front of me by an algorithm that was optimising for engagement, not for my intellectual development?

This question didn't have a comfortable answer. The honest answer was: I didn't know. And that uncertainty itself felt important.

## After 30 Days

On day 31, I reinstalled YouTube. I watched one specific video I'd been meaning to watch. Then I closed it.

I haven't deleted it again, but my relationship with it is fundamentally different. I open it when I want to watch something specific, watch that thing, and close it. The algorithm occasionally tries to hold me with a recommendation, and I am now more resistant to it because I have experienced — viscerally, in my own life — what the alternative feels like.

**The 30-day experiment gave me information about my own habits that I couldn't have obtained any other way.** I now know, from experience, that I don't need YouTube to be content. That my resting mental state is better without it. That the time it took was not time that was otherwise wasted, but time that would have gone to things I value more.

For some people — the people who ordered a Dog and Bone phone — the experiment is permanent by design. They've made the structural choice, not the willpower choice. And for them, the 30 days I found difficult are just the first 30 days of a different life.
