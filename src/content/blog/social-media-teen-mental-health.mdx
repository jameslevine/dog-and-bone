In 2023, social psychologist Jonathan Haidt published _The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness._ The book synthesised a decade of research into adolescent mental health and landed a simple, uncomfortable conclusion: **the sharp rise in teenage anxiety, depression, self-harm, and suicide that began around 2012 correlates almost precisely with the mass adoption of smartphones and social media by teenagers.**

Haidt was not the first to notice the correlation. Researchers had been tracking the data for years. But _The Anxious Generation_ brought the evidence together in a way that was impossible to dismiss, and it changed the conversation for millions of parents who had been vaguely worried about their teenager's phone use but didn't have the language or the evidence to act on their instincts.

This article is for those parents. It is a summary of what we know, why it matters, and what you can practically do about it.

## The Numbers Don't Lie

Let's start with the data, because it is striking.

From 2010 to 2023, rates of major depressive disorder among US teenagers increased by **61% for boys and 145% for girls.** In the UK, the picture is similar: NHS data shows a doubling of probable mental health disorders in children aged 6–16 between 2017 and 2023. Rates of self-harm among teenage girls have increased by more than 150% since 2010.

These are not small statistical fluctuations. These are generational crises.

The timing matters. 2012 was the year smartphones became ubiquitous among teenagers. It was the year Instagram launched on Android, making it accessible to the mass market. It was the year Facebook and Twitter became standard social infrastructure for teenagers. **The mental health data turns sharply in 2012. The technology adoption data turns sharply in 2012.** This is not coincidence.

Importantly, the effect is **much stronger for girls than for boys.** Girls are more likely to use visual social media platforms (Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok), which are more oriented around appearance, comparison, and social performance. Boys' increased depression and anxiety, while real, is partly routed through online gaming rather than social media.

## Why Social Media Is Uniquely Harmful to Teenage Girls

To understand why social media has such a disproportionate effect on teenage girls, you need to understand what adolescence involves psychologically.

Adolescence is the developmental phase in which human beings construct their identity. This process is inherently social — we define ourselves partly by how we are seen by our peer group, and peer group comparison is a central mechanism of adolescent development. Some degree of this is healthy and necessary.

Social media turbocharges this process in ways that are deeply unhealthy.

**The audience is unlimited and anonymous.** In pre-smartphone adolescence, a teenager's social comparison was bounded by their school, their neighbourhood, their town. A teenager in Manchester in 2005 compared herself to the other girls at her school. A teenager in Manchester in 2015 compared herself to every influencer, model, and perfectly curated peer on Instagram, globally, simultaneously.

**The feedback is public and quantified.** Likes, follower counts, comment sections — these convert the nebulous social anxiety of adolescence into visible, trackable metrics. You know exactly how popular you are relative to your peers, down to the like count.

**The content is curated and unrealistic.** Instagram's central activity is the presentation of a life better than the one you're actually living. Everyone looks better, thinner, more glamorous, more popular than they are in real life. Teenagers understand this intellectually and feel it emotionally as inadequacy.

**The interaction is asynchronous and performance-oriented.** Real-world social interaction is spontaneous, imperfect, and forgiving. Social media interaction requires producing content, managing a persona, and performing happiness. The labour of maintaining a social media presence is exhausting and anxiety-provoking in ways that most teenagers lack the vocabulary to describe.

## What Haidt Recommends

In _The Anxious Generation,_ Haidt makes four recommendations for society. But for individual parents, the most actionable are these:

1. **No smartphones before secondary school (approximately age 13).** Before this age, a basic phone that can call and text is sufficient for safety and communication.

2. **No social media before age 16.** This is the most controversial but best-supported recommendation. The research consistently shows that social media is most harmful to girls in the 11–14 age range, during the most formative phase of identity development.

3. **Phone-free schools.** The academic and social benefits of removing phones from schools are well-documented.

4. **More free, unstructured, unsupervised outdoor play.** The antidote to the anxious, performance-focused culture of social media is the loose, unstructured social interaction of real-world play.

For parents, recommendations 1 and 2 are the most directly actionable — and they both require controlling what's on the phone.

## Why "Just Don't Download It" Doesn't Work

Many parents try to manage this through conversation and trust. "We agreed she wouldn't download TikTok" is a common sentence heard in GP waiting rooms where parents sit with teenage daughters being treated for anxiety.

The problem is that social media apps are designed to be irresistible to exactly the demographic that is most vulnerable to them. A fourteen-year-old with a smartphone and access to the app store is being asked to consistently resist platforms that the world's most talented persuasion technologists have spent billions optimising to be as compelling as possible. The conversation is worth having, but it is not sufficient on its own.

**The only reliable solution is to make the apps unavailable.** Not password-protected or hidden — actually unavailable.

## Having the Conversation

We'd encourage parents to approach this as a conversation rather than a decree.

Teenagers — especially older teenagers — deserve to understand the reasoning. Share what you've read. Watch the Jonathan Haidt TED talk together. Discuss the data on teenage mental health. Ask your teenager what they've noticed about their own mood and anxiety levels in relation to phone use.

Many teenagers, when given the evidence and the opportunity to reflect, are ambivalent about their social media use at best. They continue it because the social cost of opting out feels high — not because they're enjoying it. **Removing the phone can give them a face-saving way to exit a dynamic they already find exhausting.**

Frame it not as punishment but as protection. Not "I don't trust you" but "I don't trust these companies to have your best interests at heart, because they demonstrably don't."

And commit to revisiting the conversation at specific milestones. A Dog and Bone Family phone for a thirteen-year-old might look different for a sixteen-year-old. The goal is not permanent restriction but protection during the most vulnerable developmental period.
